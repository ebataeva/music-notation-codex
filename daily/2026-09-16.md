1. Chord quality, scale degrees, and spelling: Dm9–G9 in D was labeled i–iv; it now reads i9–IV9, with both complete note sets. C#–E–G in D is vii°, not V. Enharmonic respelling is explicit (parser G–A#–D becomes G–Bb–D with disclosure). Cadential interpretations depend on actual adjacent chords and loop boundaries, with phrase/voicing qualifications. Sequential cello notes are not treated as full root-position chords. Borrowing and shared tones are possible interpretations, not automatic modulation or observed held voices.

### Implemented behavior

- Kept all five Theory sections. NiceGUI now displays short, concrete text based on supplied harmony, analysis, register, and style, with caveats where the notes do not establish a unique interpretation. Proposed endings and transitions are labeled as suggestions rather than detected musical events.
- Preserved all five existing `TheoryExplanation` string fields and the detailed solo explanations for existing consumers. Added `short_sections` and related `term_ids`; UI rendering still accepts older stored results without those additions.
- Solo generation receives the tonic and mode selected in the UI. Examples such as Dm9–G9 and Em9–A9 keep their actual chord qualities, extensions, relative degrees, and transposed natural sixth.
- Authored duet explanations now come from the actual fixed score, including instrument entrances, written pitches, endings, and the combined pitch collection. The UI labels these as authored duets and disables solo chord/key inputs while one is selected. Their musical material is unchanged.
- Added `/theory`, with article, tonic, and mode selection. Links from Theory cards open in a new tab with the loop's tonic, mode context, and preset; the original loop remains available and can keep playing. Standalone dictionary access defaults to C major.
- Added 19 articles: tonic, scale degrees, chord tones, triads, sevenths and ninths, modes, cadence, pedal point, ostinato, voice leading, common tone, chromatic approach, modulation, tonicization, modal borrowing, pivot chord, secondary dominant, direct transition, and same-tonic mode shift.
- Each article includes a plain definition, a notated example, audio, and listening guidance. Examples are built on request and cached by context. One `music21.Score` supplies MusicXML and MIDI, and the existing renderer synthesizes the audio from that MIDI. Cached results are copied before returning them to callers.
- Shared OSMD rendering between Loop Coach and the dictionary. Retained existing loop notation/export element IDs and the pinned OSMD version.
- Transition examples show source and destination, chord sequence, method, and evidence for a continuing or restored tonal center. Preset-specific playing advice does not redefine modulation, tonicization, borrowing, or mode change.

### Concrete musical examples

- Dm9–G9: i9–IV9 relative to D; B is the natural sixth supporting a Dorian reading. Em9–A9 transposes that relationship to E, with C# as the natural sixth.
- The ritual_tribal regression retains D natural minor where supported by its notes; preset language alone must not turn it into a Phrygian claim.
- Temporary tonicization in C: **C → E7 → Am → G7 → C**. E7–Am briefly emphasizes A, followed by a return to C.
- Establishing A minor: **C → E7 → Am → Dm → E7 → Am → Am**. The later phrase, repeated dominant–tonic arrival, and sustained destination distinguish it from the brief tonicization example.
- Modal borrowing in E Dorian: **Em → A → Am → Em**, with one borrowed Am containing C natural.
- Same-tonic mode shift: **Em → A → Am → C → Am → Em → Em**. The later phrase retains C natural across several chords and settles on Em. This example was lengthened so it is audibly different from the isolated borrowed chord; E remains the tonic.
- Common-chord and direct-transition recipes are also available. Shared notes or a single new chord alone are not presented as proof of modulation.
